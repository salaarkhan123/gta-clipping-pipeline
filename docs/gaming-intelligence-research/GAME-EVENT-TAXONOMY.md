# Game events and editorial labels

A recognizer can correctly report a goal while an editor correctly declines the clip. Label the observable event, its relationship to the person on screen and the value of the surrounding story separately. This lets evaluation distinguish three failures: missing a real event, attributing it to the wrong person, and ranking an ordinary event above a standout moment.

## Units of annotation

| Unit | Record | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Source interval | Original source ID, start/end presentation time in seconds, title and mode, language, creator group, patch/layout if known, visible game or IRL context and missing channels. | “Match, player POV, scoreboard partially cropped, no chat replay.” |
| State | Readable before/after values, including score, clock, team size, health or objective ownership where applicable. Distinguish observed zero from missing. Mark changes of match, perspective and layout. | “Tied at 1–1 with overtime indicated” provides context for an event later. |
| Physical or on-screen event | Title-specific type, first observable onset, confirmation, end if visible, actor/team role, evidence timestamps, certainty, and optional link to previous/next events. | One countable confirmed goal with a subsequent replay and kickoff. |
| Narrative beat | Setup, escalation, turning point, payoff and optional reaction. Mark which beats exist and which time span a viewer needs to understand them. | A quiet promise before a failed jump changes the meaning of the landing. |
| Editorial decision | Accept/maybe/reject; separate ratings for entertainment, clarity and boundaries; note whether original gameplay, a caster call or streamer commentary supplies the payoff. | A routine win with little tension can be rejected even at maximum certainty that the win occurred. |

Use seconds from the source presentation timeline with half-open intervals. The game clock is evidence and may stop, reset or count upward. Record an earliest/latest plausible time for blurry or delayed notifications. Record a point for a score change and an interval for the contest leading to it. Reviewers can then judge timing error and narrative completeness independently.

## Cross-title vocabulary

- **Progress and resolution.** Objective start, contest, cancel, completion, score update, round or match result, progression milestone. A change of possession or disappearance of an objective marker may need confirmation.
- **Player outcomes.** Damage, down, elimination, assist, revive, respawn, escape and return to play. Store the displayed role and attribution evidence. A team elimination and a viewpoint player's elimination can describe the same underlying episode.
- **Sequences.** Multi-elimination, comeback, close call, clutch, objective steal, comic reversal, surprise and reveal. Record the atomic events and linking evidence. A clutch hypothesis requires disadvantage or stakes before the turning point and an observed resolution. An impressive attempt can have a losing outcome.
- **Expression and audience.** Speech emphasis, silence, laughter-like audio, visible movement, chat rate change and emote concentration. Label the audible/visible response and likely source when distinguishable. Use “unclear” when a sound could come from a game, stream overlay or speaker.
- **Presentation.** Direct POV, teammate/opponent spectating, third-person broadcast, replay, menu/intermission, split-screen, edited insert, facecam-only and unknown. A stream can change mode within one clip. Preserve the timestamps of changes.

|---|---|---|
| GTA V — chase escape, failed stunt, mission reversal | Readable wanted count and minimap context before/after, rapidly closing obstacle, vehicle damage or result screen; engine pitch, sirens, impact, then voice reaction or silence. GTA has no universal team scoreboard in free roam. | A narrow escape becomes comprehensible when the viewer first sees the pursuit. Keep the maneuver and consequence. Counterexample: a cut or menu hides the stars while sirens continue. |
| GTA RP — mistaken identity, negotiation, betrayal, failed role-play plan | Establish who is speaking and what each party expects. A changed speaker, radio interruption, quiet reply, laughter, movement or server-specific downed-state HUD can anchor the turn. Counterexample: identical dispatch audio repeats as a server alert while the participants do nothing remarkable. |
| Fortnite — endgame clutch, rescue, victory or comic loss | Remaining-player count, own elimination/knock prompt, storm pressure, teammate status or victory state; shots, storm and win fanfare, then streamer response. Track player versus spectator attribution. | A low-resource player survives the final opponent and wins. Keep the visible disadvantage and final consequence. Counterexample: the camera switches to a healthy opponent and their elimination tally increases. |
| Call of Duty multiplayer — final-objective defense, last-player round win | Mode and round score, objective timer, personal feed row, alive count, win/loss banner; announcer call, countdown, game sounds, teammates reacting. | Start before the objective is about to expire and end with the outcome. Counterexample: full-screen final killcam repeats a decisive event already labeled. |

| Valorant — 1vN clutch, ace, defuse or near miss | Alive portraits, own versus assist feed decoration, timer/round score and plant/defuse/round resolution; spike beeps and announcer voice can support the sequence. | Show the numerical disadvantage before the first decisive choice, and include the result banner or failed defuse. Counterexample: one personal kill plus four team feed entries, or an assist-only banner. |
| Counter-Strike 2 — last-second defuse, round turnaround | Alive indicators, bomb/round state, new attributed kill rows, round-score change; footsteps or silence can precede a defuse sound, countdown or caster reaction. | A player chooses to stay in a defuse under pressure. Include bomb resolution, even if it explodes. Counterexample: defusing during an uncontested practice retake or watching another player do it. |
| Apex Legends — rescue-to-win, squad wipe or improbable survival | Knock/down versus confirmed elimination, ring stage, remaining squads, downed/revived teammate or champion screen; shield break, revive and announcer cues with squad comms. | A team nearly gets eliminated, recovers, and resolves the fight. Counterexample: a knock gets revived off-screen and an unrelated squad dies, or a revival playlist respawns the player automatically. |
| Minecraft — survival recovery, new milestone, build reveal or comic death | Player hearts, hotbar change, boss bar, advancement toast or death screen when available; fall, placement or advancement sound, warning by another speaker and delayed reaction. Score is often irrelevant. | Keep the high ledge or voiced prediction for a last-second recovery. For a build, establish the reveal and audience response. Counterexample: a farm produces frequent identical rewards or the toast belongs to another player in chat. |
| Rocket League — tying goal, overtime winner, save or own goal | Both scores and clock, overtime label, scorer banner/replay or goal explosion, ball movement near goal; contact, crowd sound and commentator/player response. A save can leave score unchanged. | Show the deficit or unexpired play before the goal and the update afterward. Counterexample: a replay shows the same goal from a new angle or a goal explosion on a training map leaves the match score untouched. |
| League of Legends — objective steal, base defense, team-fight reversal | Objective health/ownership if visible, kill banners, alive/death timers, scoreboard and match clock; objective/kill announcer and caster reaction. Keep summoner/side attribution when observable. | Establish contested control before the objective resolves. For a base race, show simultaneous pressure and nexus outcome. Counterexample: a neutral objective goes to the already controlling team while a caster shouts about a separate fight. |
| Rainbow Six Siege — last-operator clutch, plant under pressure, disable or attempt | Objective mode, round timer and score, alive/DBNO operator indicators, defuser active/disabled and result; activation tone, countdown, squad comms or sudden quiet. | Keep the decision to commit and the completion or interruption. Counterexample: a caster camera swaps to the opposing operator as the defuser is disabled, or animation begins and is cancelled. |
| Just Chatting — reveal, punchline, unexpected guest or callback | Speaker turn, prior question/expectation, timing pause and changed meaning in the reply. Facial motion, laughter or chat response can corroborate it. Expose “no game state” explicitly. | Preserve the question or callback and the reply people react to. Counterexample: an alert plays recorded laughter while the conversation continues unchanged. |
| IRL — unplanned interruption, physical reveal or situational humor | Observable scene change, gesture, off-camera voice, environmental audio, and the creator's interpretation. Distinguish embedded videos from events in the creator's environment. Scoreboard normally inapplicable. | Establish the setting and expectation, include the surprise and consequence even with no chat. Counterexample: sudden camera shake during routine walking with loud background traffic. |

## Perspective, replay and ambiguity

Track the **observed actor** independently of the creator and the commentator. Supported roles are self, teammate, opponent, third party and unknown. Cast footage can contain an entertaining competitor highlight without providing creator self-credit. A spectator flag and a known competitor identity can coexist. A chat author's assertion alone cannot establish the actor.

Assign one underlying event ID to a live event and its replay, then annotate separate presentation intervals. A replay may provide better visual evidence or the streamer's first entertaining reaction. Score the editorial interval on its own merits while counting the underlying gameplay event once. If the live moment is missing, label the replay as an observed presentation with unknown live timestamp. On a new match, perspective reset or montage discontinuity, link events only where evidence supports the match.

Record **unknown**, **unreadable**, **occluded**, **not applicable** and **known absent** distinctly. A pixel crop hidden by a sponsor overlay cannot serve as proof a goal did not happen. An intentionally HUD-less GTA RP conversation can still receive a high editorial rating. Mark subgroup language or transcription uncertainty when it prevents interpretation of the payoff.

## Building training and validation examples

1. Split by source VOD and creator before sampling. Group replays, reposts, edited versions and near-duplicates with the source. Existing evaluation labels remain read-only and excluded from training, development and threshold fitting.
2. Label normal play throughout the source using time sampling alongside event-enriched windows. Include quiet successes, high-energy failures, team/spectator lookalikes and edited replays. Preserve sampling probabilities so curated event prevalence is distinguishable from deployment prevalence.
3. Ask a first reviewer to timestamp observable evidence with outcome and actor. A second blinded reviewer independently scores the narrative in a context window without a model score, source popularity or platform engagement figure. Include explicit “cannot decide” and unresolved/disagree labels.
4. Adjudicate disagreements separately for the event type, attribution, boundaries and editorial rating. Preserve originals and agreement metrics. For a trusted co-capture, timestamped client events may settle score or time; only a reviewer can settle whether the clip contains its required setup and payoff.
5. Before training, verify the permission record covers the intended use and split membership. For each positive sequence retain at least one difficult comparison matched on title/mode and cue type. Pair a real ordinary kill with a meaningful kill as well as a false feed match. The first pair tests ranking; the second tests detection.

For rating scale, record a five-point standalone watchability judgment anchored at 1 (routine or unintelligible), 3 (understandable but modest payoff) and 5 (clear setup and exceptional payoff). Record a separate boundary judgment of “setup missing,” “payoff cut,” “extra dead time,” or “ready.” A reviewer can choose a losing, quiet or HUD-less moment. Also collect the evaluator’s native 1–3 publishability rating as a separate question (weak, post after trim, ready). Use that independent judgement for legacy-compatible scores.
